Designing for Alberta Winters: Smart Custom Home Choices.

Insulation, window placement, heated entries and airtight detailing — the design decisions that pay you back every cold month for the life of the home.

O
Orchid Team Updated May 2026 · 7 min read

Edmonton asks a lot of a house. Winters that dip well below minus twenty, short January days, deep snow loads and a freeze-thaw cycle that works on everything it touches — a home here either accounts for all of it in the design, or pays for ignoring it in comfort and heating bills. The good news is that the choices that make a home comfortable through an Alberta winter are made once, at the design table, and reward you quietly for decades.

Start with the envelope

Before any feature, the building envelope — the insulated, airtight shell that separates inside from out — determines how a home performs in the cold. A well-built envelope means the furnace works less, rooms stay evenly warm, and the home feels calm rather than drafty on the coldest nights. The two ideas that matter most are insulation and airtightness, and they work together: insulation slows heat from leaving, while airtightness stops cold air from sneaking in around it.

Investing in the envelope is the single highest-return decision in a cold-climate custom home. It is also the hardest to upgrade later, which is exactly why it belongs at the top of the priority list.

Insulation and airtight detailing

Good cold-climate insulation is about both quantity and continuity. The detailing that separates a warm home from an average one includes:

  • Generous, well-installed insulation in walls, attic and below the slab
  • Continuous exterior insulation to reduce thermal bridging through the framing
  • A well-sealed air barrier with careful attention to penetrations and transitions
  • Properly managed vapour control suited to our climate
  • Controlled ventilation so a tight home still breathes and stays healthy

An airtight home needs deliberate ventilation — typically a heat-recovery ventilator — so it stays fresh and dry without throwing away the warmth you paid to keep. Tight and well-ventilated is the goal; tight alone is a mistake.

You design the envelope once and live with it for thirty winters. It is the rare decision where doing it right the first time is also the cheapest.

Windows and where they face

Windows are where heat most easily escapes, so both their quality and their placement matter. High-performance windows — well-sealed, with good glazing suited to the cold — hold warmth far better than the single- and early double-pane units in many older Edmonton homes. Placement matters too: thoughtful south-facing glazing captures low winter sun for free heat and daylight on short days, while restraint on cold north and wind-exposed faces limits heat loss. Good design balances the view you want with the performance you need.

Heated entries, garages and the daily realities

Some of the best winter design is about everyday life, not just thermodynamics. A proper mudroom or boot room handles snow, salt and wet gear without spreading them through the house. An attached, insulated garage keeps vehicles and the path to the door out of the weather. Considered details — in-floor heating at entries, generous overhangs that shed snow away from doors, and durable, slip-aware surfaces — turn a long winter from a daily battle into a non-event.

Heating and the snow load above you

A right-sized, efficient heating system in a well-built envelope keeps a home comfortable without overworking. Because the envelope does most of the work, the mechanical system can be smaller and cheaper to run than the same system in a leaky house. Above your head, the roof has to carry Alberta's snow and shed meltwater cleanly — sensible roof design, proper attic ventilation and good flashing prevent the ice dams and leaks that plague poorly detailed homes after a heavy winter.
