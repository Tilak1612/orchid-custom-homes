What to Know Before Buying a Lot in Edmonton.

Zoning, grade, servicing, frontage and resale — the pre-purchase checks that separate a great building lot from an expensive mistake.

O
Orchid Team Updated May 2026 · 7 min read

A lot is the one decision in a custom home that you cannot undo. You can revise a floor plan, change a finish or swap a fixture, but you cannot move the land. That is why the most valuable hours in any build happen before the offer — walking the parcel, reading the title and understanding what the City will and will not allow you to do there. Here is what experienced buyers check before they commit in Edmonton.

Start with zoning, not the view

The zoning on a lot defines what you can build — height, setbacks, site coverage, how many units and what kind. Edmonton has been moving toward a simpler, more permissive approach to small-scale residential across its mature neighbourhoods, which has widened the options on many infill lots. But every parcel is governed by its specific designation, and a lovely lot zoned for something other than what you have in mind is not the bargain it appears to be.

Before you fall for the trees or the street, confirm the lot's current designation and what it permits. If your plans depend on a rezoning or a variance, treat that as a real project with its own timeline and risk — not a formality.

Grade, drainage and what's under the soil

Edmonton's river-valley topography means grade matters. A lot that slopes the wrong way, sits low relative to its neighbours, or drains toward the house can add foundation, landscaping and engineering costs that never appear in the listing price. On infill and teardown sites, what is below grade matters too: old foundations, fill of unknown origin, buried tanks or services can all surface once excavation begins.

A geotechnical look and a careful read of the existing grades are cheap insurance against an expensive surprise after possession.

Servicing and utilities

Not all lots are equally ready to build on. The questions worth answering early:

  • Are water, sanitary and storm services already to the property line, or will they need to be brought in?
  • Is the electrical service adequate, and where does it enter?
  • Is natural gas available at the lot?
  • On a teardown, what will demolition, disconnection and abandonment of old services cost?
  • Are there easements or rights-of-way that limit where you can place the home?

Servicing gaps are recoverable, but they are rarely cheap, and they belong in your budget before you decide what the lot is worth to you.

The best lot is not the prettiest one — it's the one whose constraints you understand completely before you sign.

Frontage, dimensions and what fits

Frontage and lot dimensions quietly govern your whole design. A narrow lot can be wonderful, but it dictates garage placement, the width of the home, window opportunities and how light moves through the plan. Before you assume a particular home will fit, sketch the buildable envelope — the area left after setbacks and easements — and make sure your must-haves live comfortably inside it.

Trees, neighbours and the streetscape

Mature trees are part of what makes Edmonton's established neighbourhoods desirable, and some are protected or sit on shared boundaries. Understand which trees stay, what removal involves, and how the existing streetscape shapes expectations for your build. A home that respects its context is also easier to permit and, usually, easier to sell.

Resale is a lot decision too

Even if you plan to stay for decades, buy the lot with a clear head about its long-term value. Location, walkability, the trajectory of the neighbourhood and the calibre of surrounding homes all feed into what your finished home will be worth. The land is usually the most durable part of the investment — the part that holds value when finishes age.
