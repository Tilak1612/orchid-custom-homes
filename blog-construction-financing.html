Understanding construction financing & draw schedules.

A build mortgage works nothing like the loan you used to buy a finished house — here is how draws are released by stage, and how to plan for the cash-flow gaps in between.

O
Orchid Team Updated May 2026 · 8 min read

When you buy a finished home, the mortgage is simple: the lender advances the full amount at possession and you start making payments. A custom build is different. The house does not exist yet, so a lender will not hand over the money all at once — there is nothing to take security against until the structure is in the ground. Instead, funds are released in stages as the home is built. Understanding that rhythm, and the cash-flow gaps it creates, is one of the most important things a first-time builder in Edmonton can learn before signing anything.

This guide explains how a construction (or "draw") mortgage works, what triggers each release, and where homeowners most often get caught short. It is general information, not financial advice — your lender and mortgage broker will confirm the exact terms for your situation.

How a build mortgage differs from a regular one

A standard purchase mortgage is a single lump sum secured by a home that already exists. A construction mortgage is structured around progress. The lender approves a total amount up front, but advances it in instalments — called draws — that line up with completed stages of construction. You typically pay interest only on the funds advanced so far, which keeps early payments lower, then convert to a conventional mortgage once the home is complete and occupancy is granted.

Because the lender is taking on more risk during construction, expect more documentation: fixed-price contracts, detailed budgets, a building permit, proof of insurance, and an appraisal based on the finished plans rather than the current vacant lot.

How draws are released by stage

Most lenders release money against verified construction milestones. Before each advance, an appraiser or inspector visits the site to confirm the work is genuinely complete to that stage. A typical draw schedule in Alberta moves through something like the following:

  • Foundation — excavation, footings and foundation poured, backfilled.
  • Lock-up — framing complete, roof on, windows and exterior doors installed.
  • Drywall / mechanical — wiring, plumbing, heating roughed in, insulation and drywall up.
  • Completion — finishes, cabinetry, flooring, fixtures, final grade and occupancy.

Each release is reduced by a holdback the lender retains until the next stage is verified, so you rarely receive 100% of a stage's cost at the moment that stage finishes. That gap is normal and built into the system — but you have to plan for it.

The single most common surprise for first-time builders is the holdback: you have paid trades for work that is done, but the matching draw has not fully landed yet. A short-term cushion bridges that gap.

Holdbacks and the builders' lien

Alberta's lien legislation requires a portion of each payment to contractors to be held back for a set period after work is substantially complete. This protects against unpaid subtrades placing a lien on your title. Your lender accounts for this when sizing each draw, which is part of why advances trail the work slightly. Confirm the current holdback percentage and release timing with your lawyer or lender — the figures are set by provincial rules that can change.

Budgeting for the gaps

Because draws arrive after stages complete, you will often need to cover costs before the matching money is in hand. Practical ways to stay liquid:

  • Keep a contingency of roughly 5–10% of the build budget for overages and timing (illustrative — confirm with your broker).
  • Budget for interest carrying costs during construction, plus any rent or existing mortgage you are paying in parallel.
  • Line up the appraisal and inspection early for each stage so draws are not delayed by scheduling.
  • Understand which soft costs — permits, surveys, utility connections — your lender will and will not finance.

Edmonton-specific notes

Local realities matter. Winter excavation and grade restrictions can shift stage timing, which in turn shifts when draws are available. Permit and inspection turnaround at the City of Edmonton affects the foundation and occupancy stages in particular. And on an infill lot, demolition, lot servicing and site access can add early costs that come before the first draw — so map those out with your builder and lender from the start.
