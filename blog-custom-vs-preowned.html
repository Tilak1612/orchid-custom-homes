Custom Home vs. Pre-Owned in Edmonton: The Real Math.

Once you add up lot cost, financing, energy efficiency and the renovations an older home will eventually need, the gap between building new and buying resale is usually smaller than buyers expect.

O
Orchid Team Updated May 2026 · 8 min read

Few decisions in a home purchase feel as fraught as the choice between building new and buying resale. The sticker price of a pre-owned house in a mature Edmonton neighbourhood almost always looks lower than the all-in cost of a custom build — and for many buyers that is where the comparison ends. But a fair comparison has to run the numbers over the years you will actually own the home, not just the day you sign. When you do that, the math gets a lot more interesting.

The sticker price is only the start

A resale listing shows you one number. A custom build shows you several — land, design, permits, construction and finishing — and people instinctively compare the single resale figure to the full build figure. That is rarely apples to apples. The pre-owned price already bundles in a lot, existing services, landscaping and a finished kitchen; the build price is itemised, which makes it look larger even when the delivered value is comparable.

A cleaner way to compare is on a cost-per-finished-square-foot basis, adjusted for the condition and age of the resale home. An older Edmonton bungalow priced attractively may carry tens of thousands in deferred work behind the drywall — and that is the figure that closes the gap.

The hidden costs of an older home

Edmonton's housing stock spans more than a century. A charming home from the 1960s or 70s often comes with systems near the end of their service life. Before you fall for the character, budget honestly for what the inspection report tends to surface:

  • Furnace, hot-water tank and HVAC nearing replacement
  • Original or aluminum wiring and undersized electrical panels
  • Single-pane or failed double-pane windows that bleed heat all winter
  • Thin or settled attic insulation well below current standards
  • Aging shingles, sub-grade drainage and the occasional poly-B plumbing line
  • Kitchens and bathrooms a renovation or two behind today's expectations

None of these are deal-breakers on their own. Together, though, they can quietly add a renovation budget on top of the purchase price — and you live through the disruption while you spend it.

The resale price is what you pay to get in. The renovation backlog is what you pay to make it the home you actually wanted.

How the financing differs

This is where the two paths diverge most. Buying resale is a familiar transaction: a deposit, a standard mortgage, possession in 30 to 90 days. A custom build runs on construction financing, where funds are released in stages — a draw schedule — as the home is framed, closed-in, finished and completed. You carry the loan as it grows rather than all at once, and you typically need to budget for interim interest and a deposit during the build window before your standard mortgage takes over at possession.

It is not harder, but it is different, and it rewards planning. Knowing how draws are released helps you align your own cash flow with the build timeline so there are no surprises mid-project.

Energy efficiency and the long run

In a climate that swings from minus thirty to plus thirty, the building envelope is not a luxury — it is an operating cost. A new custom home built to current Alberta energy expectations, with continuous insulation, high-performance windows and airtight detailing, simply costs less to heat than a comparable home built decades ago. Over a fifteen- or twenty-year hold, those monthly savings are real money, and they arrive whether or not natural-gas prices behave.

A pre-owned home can be upgraded to similar performance, of course — but retrofitting insulation and replacing windows after the fact is almost always more expensive and more disruptive than building it right the first time.

So which actually wins?

There is no universal answer, and anyone who gives you one is selling something. Pre-owned can be the right call when you need to move quickly, love an established streetscape, or find a home that has already been thoughtfully updated. Building custom tends to win when you plan to stay, value efficiency and low maintenance, and want a floor plan shaped around how your household actually lives rather than how someone else lived in 1978.

For many Edmonton buyers, the honest takeaway is this: the up-front premium on a custom build is smaller than it looks, and the long-term value — lower running costs, fewer surprise repairs, a layout that fits — often tips the decision once the full picture is on the table.
